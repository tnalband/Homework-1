# Question 7: DFA's small and value stock strategies

**DFA's strategies.** DFA began with a "small-stock" fund (now the U.S. Micro Cap Portfolio) holding stocks below the 20th NYSE percentile, then added the U.S. Small Cap and Small XM portfolios and small-cap funds abroad. After Fama and French's 1992 paper it added value funds, buying high-BE/ME stocks and avoiding low-BE/ME "growth" or "glamour" stocks. It holds them passively and diversified and adds value through trading.

**Efficient-market view.** Banz found that small stocks beat large ones from 1926 to the late 1970s. Fama and French found that high-BE/ME stocks beat low-BE/ME stocks while high "beta" did not earn higher returns ("beta is dead"). Efficient-market enthusiasts read this as compensation for risk: value stocks are "distressed" firms with poor recent profitability that "could easily topple into ruin." SMB and HML, the size and value factors of the Fama–French three-factor model, capture this risk. The value effect held in 1926–62 data and in nearly every country, and value returns are highly correlated across countries, so the risk cannot be diversified away.

**Behavioral view.** Others argued that small and value stocks were not risky enough to justify their returns and pointed to investors' "common irrational biases." Glamour stocks are overhyped and overpriced, while value stocks are out of favor. Skeptics also called the premia a fluke of limited data, or said they "went away" once DFA and others traded on them.

**What DFA believes.** DFA believes in efficient markets and takes the risk side: value and small stocks outperform "because they were riskier." It prefers "growth" to "glamour" because the overhyped-stocks story "did not fit with its efficient markets views." It stayed the course through the 1980s and 1990s, and the over-80% HML return in 2000–01 "justif[ied] the faith" of its investors.
