# Question 7: DFA's small and value stock strategies

DFA began with a "small-stock" fund (now the U.S. Micro Cap Portfolio) holding stocks below the 20th NYSE percentile, then added the U.S. Small Cap and Small XM portfolios and small-cap funds abroad. After Fama and French's 1992 paper it added value funds, buying high-BE/ME stocks and avoiding low-BE/ME "growth" or "glamour" stocks. It holds them passively and diversified and adds value through trading. The case gives two competing explanations for why such stocks have earned higher returns.

Banz found that small stocks outperformed large stocks over 1926 to the late 1970s, and Fama and French found that high-BE/ME stocks consistently beat low-BE/ME stocks, while high-"beta" stocks did not earn higher returns (the "beta is dead" result). Efficient-market enthusiasts read this as compensation for risk: small and value stocks outperformed "for the only reason any asset consistently outperforms in a rational, efficient market: because they were riskier." Value stocks have low prices despite substantial assets on the books because they are companies with poor recent performance and profitability, "distressed" firms that "could easily topple into ruin." Fama and French's three-factor model captures this with SMB and HML. Sensitivity to these two factors, like sensitivity to the market, constitutes risk, and together with the market they explain much of the variation in stock returns. The effect held in Fama, French and Davis's 1926–62 data and in virtually every country in the Morgan Stanley data, so it was not a fluke. Value-growth returns were highly correlated across countries, which means a global value portfolio could not remove the risk, and in some years growth beat value across many countries, as risk would suggest.

Behavioralists, however, countered that small and value stocks "did not appear to be nearly risky enough to justify their high average returns," and used evidence from psychology to argue that the effects reflect investors' "common irrational biases." On this view, "growth" (glamour) stocks are overhyped and glamorized, so investors overpay for them, while value stocks are out of favor and underpriced. Skeptics also called the premia a fluke or a product of data problems, since the research used only about 25 years of data from the United States. They said small stocks lagged in the 1980s because the S&P 500 had become the benchmark and investors favored large stocks, and others said DFA started at the wrong moment and that once DFA and other funds traded on the size effect, "the effect went away."

DFA itself sides with the efficient-market view and believes small and value stocks outperform because they are riskier. It prefers "growth" to "glamour" because the overhyped-stocks story "did not fit with its efficient markets views." It stayed the course through the 1980s and 1990s, and the over-80% HML return in 2000–01 "justif[ied] the faith" of its investors.
