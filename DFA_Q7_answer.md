# Question 7: DFA's small and value stock strategies

**DFA's strategies.** DFA started in 1981 with a "small-stock" fund holding stocks below the 20th percentile of NYSE market capitalization (the 9th and 10th size deciles), now called the U.S. Micro Cap Portfolio. It then added the U.S. Small Cap Portfolio (6–10 deciles) and the U.S. Small XM Portfolio (stocks eligible for 6–10 but not 9–10), and small-cap funds for the U.K., Japan, Europe and the Pacific Rim. After Fama and French's 1992 paper, DFA launched a U.S. Small Value fund, buying stocks with a high book-to-market ratio (BE/ME) and avoiding low-BE/ME "growth" or "glamour" stocks. International and large-cap value funds followed. DFA holds these stocks passively and diversified, without stock picking, and tries to add value through its trading.

**What efficient-market enthusiasts say.** Banz found that small stocks outperformed large stocks over 1926 to the late 1970s, and Fama and French found that high-BE/ME stocks consistently beat low-BE/ME stocks, while high-"beta" stocks did not earn higher returns (the "beta is dead" result). An efficient-market theorist reads this as compensation for risk. In their words, small and value stocks outperformed "for the only reason any asset consistently outperforms in a rational, efficient market: because they were riskier." Value stocks have low prices despite substantial assets on the books because they are companies with poor recent performance and profitability, "distressed" firms that "could easily topple into ruin." Fama and French's three-factor model captures this with SMB (small minus big) and HML (high minus low BE/ME). Sensitivity to these two factors, like sensitivity to the market, constitutes risk, and together with the market they explain much of the variation in stock returns. Averages for 1926–2001 were SMB 2.4% and HML 4.7% a year, with standard deviations of 11.7% and 12.5% (Exhibit 6). The effect held in Fama, French and Davis's 1926–62 data and in virtually every country in the Morgan Stanley data, so it was not a fluke. Value-growth returns were highly correlated across countries, which means a global value portfolio could not remove the risk, and in some years growth beat value across many countries, as risk would suggest. Small-company profitability was also weak in the 1980s and early 1990s, so the poor returns then were not just a loss of favor. The poor stretches fit the risk story, and so did 2000–01, when value stocks massively outperformed with HML returning over 80%.

**What behavioralists say.** Others countered that small and value stocks "did not appear to be nearly risky enough to justify their high average returns," and used evidence from psychology to argue that the effects reflect investors' "common irrational biases." On this view, "growth" (glamour) stocks are overhyped and glamorized, so investors overpay for them, while value stocks are out of favor and underpriced. The 1990s fit this: as tech stocks soared, the higher growth stock prices climbed, the more tempting low-priced value stocks looked to value investors. Skeptics also called the premia a fluke or a product of data problems, since the research used only about 25 years of data from the United States. They said small stocks lagged in the 1980s because the S&P 500 had become the benchmark and investors favored large stocks. Others said DFA started at the wrong moment, and that once DFA and other funds traded on the size effect, "the effect went away."

**What DFA believes.** DFA believes in efficient markets and takes the risk side. It argues that value stocks beat growth stocks because they are riskier, and it prefers the term "growth" to "glamour" because "the idea that low BE/ME stocks performed poorly because they were overhyped and glamorized did not fit with its efficient markets views." It acknowledges that "the source of this risk was unclear" but regards the idea as plausible. DFA stays with the strategies through poor periods. It kept its small-stock fund through the 1980s drought and avoided the high-tech growth stocks in the 1990s. It saw 2000–01, when value returned over 80% on HML, as "justifying the faith" of its long-term investors.
