# Question 8: Trading costs of small, value stocks and how DFA manages them

**Trading costs.** Buying the stock of a very small company "could move the stock's price substantially," the liquidity problem that kept mutual funds in large stocks. When funds arrive faster than sellers, DFA must "work the order" in the open market, which cost about 0.58% in 2001. DFA also faces adverse selection (the "lemons" problem), since a stock may be for sale only because the seller knows something negative. Price pressure is another cost: if the seller dumps more stock after DFA buys, the price falls. Matching the index exactly would force DFA to pay premiums, and tax-managed funds add transaction costs.

**How DFA manages them.** DFA absorbs other participants' selling demand, buying large blocks at a discount instead of bidding in the open market. In 2001, 36% of Small Cap purchases were block trades at an average 3.33% discount, and the weighted average over all orders was a 0.83% discount (Exhibit 10). This helped DFA beat typical small-stock indexes by about 200 basis points a year. To limit adverse selection, DFA avoids trades just before earnings announcements and stocks with recent insider sales, though it does no fundamental analysis. It deals with trustworthy brokers, using a "penalty box" for those who withhold information, and insists the seller offer the entire block, in return for taking it all and keeping the sale secret. It buys more of an overweight stock only at a bigger discount, to keep tracking error low. It rarely sells, only when a stock no longer fits, such as moving from Micro Cap to Small XM. It offers under 25% of daily volume at a time and pays large withdrawals "in kind."
