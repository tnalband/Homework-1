# Question 8: Trading costs of small, value stocks and how DFA manages them

**Trading costs.** Buying the stock of a very small company "could move the stock's price substantially." This liquidity problem is the main reason mutual funds historically focused on large stocks. If funds come in faster than DFA can find sellers, it must buy in the open market and "work the order," which cost about 0.58% in 2001. DFA also faces the adverse selection ("lemons") problem: a stock may be for sale, or only at a given price, because the seller knows something negative that the market does not. A related cost is price pressure. If the seller dumps another block right after DFA buys, the price falls immediately. Matching the index exactly would also force DFA to be a determined buyer, "paying premiums for stocks instead of getting discounts," and DFA's tax-managed funds add costs, since profitable block trades may have to be passed up to avoid dividend-paying stocks and loss harvesting needs extra transactions.

**How DFA manages them.** DFA turns the liquidity problem into an advantage by absorbing other participants' selling demand. It buys large blocks from eager sellers at a discount instead of bidding in the open market. In 2001, 36% of Small Cap purchases (59% for Micro Cap) were block trades at an average discount of 3.33%, and the weighted average over all orders was a 0.83% discount (2.13% for Micro Cap) (Exhibit 10). Taking the discount and avoiding adverse selection let DFA beat typical small-stock indexes by about 200 basis points a year.

DFA manages adverse selection with a list of questions for each trade. *Is there important information we don't know?* It will not close a trade just before an earnings announcement, it checks news sources, and it avoids stocks with recent insider sales, though it does no fundamental analysis. *Is this someone we are comfortable trading with?* It deals with brokers that fully disclose what they know, and its "penalty box" bars any broker that withheld information. *Is this the entire block?* DFA insists that sellers offer their whole position so no further sale pushes the price down, and in return it takes the entire block and keeps the sale secret. *Does the trade increase or reduce diversification?* DFA buys more of an overweight stock only at a bigger discount, which keeps tracking error against the small-stock index low. *How big a discount can we obtain?* This ranges from minutes to two weeks of negotiation, and DFA will accept more adverse selection risk or slightly less diversification for a large enough discount.

On the selling side, DFA rarely needs to sell because assets grew steadily. It sells only when a stock no longer fits the portfolio, for example when a small stock becomes large. Such stocks can go from the Micro Cap Portfolio to the Small XM Portfolio, and by then they are usually liquid. It offers small amounts, typically under 25% of daily volume, and it can pay large withdrawals "in kind" so redemptions do not force sales.
