# Question 9: The Fama–French three-factor model

In their 1993 paper, Fama and French built two new portfolios to go with the market: SMB (small minus big), long small stocks and short big stocks, and HML (high minus low BE/ME), long value stocks and short growth stocks. A stock's or fund's excess return is then explained by its sensitivity to three factors, the market (beta), SMB and HML, so R<sub>i</sub> – R<sub>f</sub> = a + b·(R<sub>M</sub> – R<sub>f</sub>) + s·SMB + h·HML + e. The model summarizes the relative performance of small versus big and value versus growth portfolios and measures how much a portfolio moves with each, and it explains a great deal of the variation in stock prices.

The rational argument behind the model follows from this explanatory power. Because the factors explain so much of the common variation in stock returns, Fama and French argued that they are tied to real economic risk. In an efficient market an asset earns higher average returns only if it is riskier, and sensitivity to SMB and HML, like sensitivity to the market, constitutes risk. Value stocks are "distressed" firms with poor recent profitability, so their higher average returns (HML averaged 4.7% a year and SMB 2.4%, Exhibit 6) are compensation for bearing that risk, not a free lunch.

This also explains the limited role of beta. Beta alone fails, since high-beta stocks did not consistently earn more than low-beta stocks (the "beta is dead" result), and it cannot explain the size and book-to-market effects. But it stays in the model as the market factor, and market risk earned a 7.9% premium over T-bills (Exhibit 6). Beta is therefore necessary but not sufficient, and it does not distinguish small or value stocks from the rest.

Finally, DFA has good reasons not to build its products on macroeconomic variables. SMB and HML are observable, tradable portfolios, so DFA can build funds that load on them directly, measure their exposure and evaluate results, whereas macro variables arrive slowly, are noisy and give no portfolio to hold. Linking returns to macro variables would also require forecasting the economy, which clashes with DFA's passive, efficient-market belief that prices already reflect public information. And the size and value premia have held across more than 75 years and many countries without any macro model, so DFA can rely on the factors even though their ultimate economic source is unclear.
