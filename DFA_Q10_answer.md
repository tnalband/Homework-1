# Question 10: Justifying DFA in an efficient market

Believing in efficient markets does not mean believing that all stocks earn the same return. It means that no one can consistently beat the market on a risk-adjusted basis, and that higher average returns come only from bearing more risk. DFA's size and BE/ME strategies fit this view because they are not a claim of free returns: small and value stocks are riskier, so investors who hold them should earn more over the long run. DFA's clients, mostly tax-exempt pension funds, endowments and the wealthy clients of its advisors, are long-term investors who can bear that risk, and DFA's 2000–01 results, when value massively outperformed, justified the faith of those who stayed.

DFA also does not rely on stock picking. It does no fundamental analysis and holds diversified, passive portfolios of broad small and value segments, so it is not trying to outguess the market. Its edge comes from implementation. Its traders absorb the selling demand of others and take discounts on block trades while avoiding adverse selection, which helped it beat small-stock indexes by about 200 basis points a year. This return is payment for supplying liquidity, not a bet that prices are wrong, so it is consistent with efficient markets. Skilled trading is one of the three core beliefs DFA was founded on, alongside efficient markets and sound academic research.

Finally, its fees are reasonable for what it does. At 35–50 basis points, DFA charges less than the median active small-cap fund (69–85 basis points, Exhibit 5), while being above pure index funds, which fits a passive fund that still claims to add value. In short, DFA justifies its existence as a low-cost, research-based way to hold the size and value risks, with trading skill that lowers the cost of doing so.
