# DFA: Answers to Questions 7–10

**7. Size and value: rational vs. behavioral views.** Small stocks beat large ones over 1942–91 (16.2% vs. 12.9% a year for the S&P 500), and in 1964–91 small high-BE/ME stocks returned 18.0% against 10.2% for small low-BE/ME stocks (1993 Exs. 3, 11). Efficient-market theorists say these premia must compensate for risk that beta misses. High-BE/ME firms are distressed, with low and falling earnings, while low-BE/ME firms are consistently profitable (Ex. 10). Small firms have more volatile earnings. Value returns are also correlated across countries, so the risk cannot be diversified away, and the premia vanish for years at a time (SMB was –0.9% in 1982–93). Behavioralists read the same facts as mispricing: investors extrapolate growth and overpay for "glamour" stocks, and small stocks are neglected. They note that value stocks were no more volatile than growth stocks (small: 26.0% vs. 30.0%), so the extra return looks like an error rather than a reward. DFA takes the risk view, calling high-BE/ME stocks "stressed" and rejecting the glamour story as inconsistent with efficient markets.

**8. Trading costs.** Small stocks are costly to trade: the bid–ask spread is 11.32% in decile 10 against 0.48% in decile 1, daily dollar volume is $41,000 against $25.8 million, and block sellers give up 2.94% (1993 Exs. 4–5). Sellers may also know something the market does not (adverse selection), and DFA's size limits capacity. DFA instead acts as a liquidity provider, absorbing large blocks from impatient sellers at a discount. In 2001, 36% of purchases were discounted blocks at –3.33%, so the overall cost was –0.83%, about 200 bp a year better than small-stock indexes (2002 Ex. 10). It manages adverse selection by avoiding stocks with imminent news or insider sales, trading with trusted brokers (with a "penalty box" for the rest), and requiring sellers to offer their entire position. Hold ranges, in-kind redemptions and selling in small pieces limit other costs. The discounted share of purchases has fallen from 80% in 1992 to 36% in 2001.

**9. The three-factor model.** The model is R<sub>i</sub> – R<sub>f</sub> = a + b(MKT) + s·SMB + h·HML + e. In the 25-portfolio regressions, b is near 1 for all of them, s and h vary widely, R² is 0.84–0.96, and 23 of 25 intercepts are zero (Addendum Exh. 4). The rational argument is that SMB and HML capture common, undiversifiable variation tied to earnings distress, so their premia are fair compensation. Beta is not useful for explaining differences across equity portfolios. Alone it has no relation to returns, and it still only sets the stock-vs-bills mix. As a DFA representative, I would say we avoid macro variables for four reasons. They are measured infrequently and noisily, theory does not say which are priced, and we cannot build a portfolio "long GDP growth." Using them would also require economic forecasting, which is the active management we reject. Factor portfolios are tradable and observed monthly, and they capture the underlying state variables even though we cannot yet name them.

**10. Justifying DFA in an efficient market.** Efficiency does not mean equal expected returns. It means no risk-adjusted alpha from picking or timing, and expected return follows risk. DFA never claims free returns. It argues that the market portfolio has "no special role," so long-horizon investors such as pensions and endowments can rationally tilt toward small and value for higher expected return. Moving from 100% S&P 500 to 40% S&P, 40% big value, 10% small and 10% small value raises returns from 9.9% to 13.0% while the standard deviation rises only from 16.5% to 17.5% (Addendum Exh. 6). DFA earns its fee through implementation: pure, diversified factor exposure, low turnover, no research costs, and a liquidity-provision return from block trades. Its 35–50 bp fees are below the 69–85 bp median for active small-cap funds (2002 Ex. 5). The risks are real, since small and value lagged for years, but DFA's clients accept that tradeoff.
