## 7. Size and value strategies: rational vs. behavioral views

Small stocks have beaten large stocks over long periods. From 1942 to 1991 small stocks (deciles 9–10) returned 16.2% a year against 12.9% for the S&P 500 (Ex. 3), and Banz found the same pattern for 1926 to the late 1970s. Fama and French found that high book-to-market (BE/ME) stocks beat low BE/ME stocks in 1963–90 while beta explained nothing. In 1964–91 small high-BE/ME stocks returned 18.0% a year against 10.2% for small low-BE/ME stocks, and large high-BE/ME stocks returned 14.3% against 9.8% for large low-BE/ME stocks (Ex. 11). The value effect also held in the 1926–62 data that Fama, French and Davis compiled by hand, and in nearly every country in the MSCI data (2002 case).

Efficient-market theorists argue that in an efficient market any persistent excess return must be compensation for risk. Beta cannot explain these premia, so size and book-to-market must stand in for other risks that investors price. For value, the evidence is in the earnings. High-BE/ME firms are relatively distressed, with low and falling earnings on book equity until the year they are ranked, while low-BE/ME firms are consistently profitable (Ex. 10; Addendum Exhs. 2–3). Small high-BE/ME firms lose money on average in some years, so holders bear the risk of a distress state in which these firms suffer together. Fama and French also found that value-minus-growth returns are correlated across countries, so the risk cannot be diversified away internationally, and there were years when growth beat value in many countries at once (2002 case). For size, small firms have more volatile earnings and weaker profitability (Addendum Exh. 3), and their returns are more volatile too (a 27.3% standard deviation for CRSP 6–10 against 16.1% for the S&P 500, Ex. 11). Finally, the premia are not constant. SMB averaged –0.9% a year in 1982–93 (2002 Ex. 6), value lagged in the late 1990s, and HML then returned over 80% in 2000–01. A premium that disappears for years is what one would expect from a reward for bearing risk.

Behavioralists read the same facts as mispricing. Investors extrapolate recent growth and overpay for "glamour" stocks, which are later disappointing, while they shun distressed "value" firms. The 2002 case mentions "new theories that used evidence from psychology research" along these lines. Small firms may be neglected, under-researched and illiquid, and some argue that the size effect is data mining or that it vanished once it became widely known, citing the long small-stock drought of the 1980s. Behavioralists also point out that the value premium does not come with higher volatility. Small high-BE/ME stocks had a 26.0% standard deviation against 30.0% for low BE/ME, and for large stocks the figures were 17.3% against 17.9% (Ex. 11). Higher return without higher volatility looks like mispricing to them.

DFA sides with the risk view. The 1993 Addendum says high-BE/ME firms would be better called "stressed" or "stretched" than "value," and low-BE/ME firms "healthy" rather than "growth," because the extra return rewards distress risk. The 2002 case says DFA rejected the "glamour" explanation because the idea that low-BE/ME stocks do badly because they are overhyped "did not fit its efficient markets views." DFA concedes that the underlying risk has "yet to be identified" (Addendum), but it does not need to win the debate to run the strategy. It only needs the premia to persist and its clients to be able to bear the risk.

## 8. Trading costs of small, value stocks and how DFA manages them

Small and value stocks are expensive to trade. The bid–ask spread is 0.48% of price in the largest size decile and 11.32% in the smallest, more than 23 times as much, and average daily dollar volume per issue is only $41,000 in decile 10 against $25.8 million in decile 1 (Ex. 4). Buying moves the price, and the average seller's cost on a block trade is 2.94% (Ex. 5). There is also an adverse-selection problem, because a seller who offers a block may know something the market does not. There is a leakage problem too, because if the seller dumps the rest of its position after DFA buys, the price falls. Capacity is limited as well. DFA already owned over 3% of the smallest two deciles in 1993, 6–9% of many individual stocks, and over 5% of many small firms by 2002. Stocks also drift across size and BE/ME boundaries, which forces further trades.

DFA turns this weakness into an edge by acting as a liquidity provider instead of a liquidity taker. Rather than bidding in the open market, it absorbs large blocks from impatient sellers and demands a discount. In 1993 its computer system screened about 600 block inquiries a day from 100 brokers, and in 2002 it executed about 20 of roughly 1,000 potential trades a day. In 2001, 36% of small-cap purchases were discounted blocks at a cost of –3.33% (a gain), against +0.58% for orders worked in the market, so the overall cost was –0.83% (2002 Ex. 10). DFA credits this, together with avoiding adverse selection, for beating small-stock indexes by about 200 basis points a year.

To protect itself, DFA avoids stocks with earnings announcements or other news imminent, and stocks with recent insider sales. It does no fundamental research, because it believes the price already reflects public information and it only needs to rule out negative private information. It trades with brokers that have been candid with it, and it keeps a "penalty box" of brokers who withheld information. It also insists that a seller offer its whole position so no further sale follows, and its reputation for secrecy makes sellers willing to disclose that. DFA limits turnover with hold ranges: Exhibit 7 shows buy, hold and sell bands, and a stock is sold only when it strays well beyond its target range. It will buy more of an already overweight stock only for a bigger discount, so diversification and tracking error stay under control. When it sells, it offers less than 25% of daily volume at a time, and it can pay large redemptions in kind. Graduating stocks can be passed from the Micro Cap Portfolio to the Small XM Portfolio.

The strategy has limits. As DFA has grown, the share of purchases made at a discount has fallen from 80% in 1992 to 36% in 2001 (2002 Ex. 10), and capacity constraints are the reason DFA pushed large clients toward the 6-7-8 strategy. DFA's fees of 35–50 basis points (2002 Ex. 5) are justified only if the trading gains keep covering them.

## 9. The Fama–French three-factor model

The three-factor model says that a portfolio's excess return is R<sub>i</sub> – R<sub>f</sub> = a + b(R<sub>M</sub> – R<sub>f</sub>) + s·SMB + h·HML + e. SMB is the return on small stocks minus big stocks and HML is the return on high-BE/ME stocks minus low-BE/ME stocks. The coefficients b, s and h measure the portfolio's exposure to market, size and value risk. In the Addendum's regressions on 25 size/BE/ME portfolios (Exh. 4), b is close to 1.0 for almost all portfolios, s runs from about +1.2 for the smallest stocks to about –0.15 for the largest, and h runs from negative for low BE/ME to +0.6 to +0.7 for high BE/ME. R² is 0.84–0.96, and 23 of 25 intercepts are statistically zero, so the three factors explain most of both the variation and the average level of returns.

The rational argument is that if exposure to SMB and HML explains average returns, and these exposures are tied to real economic risk, then the premia are fair compensation. The factors capture common variation in returns that diversification cannot remove, and small and high-BE/ME firms share an earnings distress pattern (Ex. 10; Addendum Exh. 2). The results also appear in 1926–62 data and across countries, which makes a data-mining explanation unlikely.

Beta is not useful for explaining why one equity portfolio earns more than another. Alone it has no relation to average returns, and after adjusting for size, low-beta stocks earned more than high-beta stocks (Appendix A1). It still has a role inside the model as the market exposure, which determines how much to hold in stocks rather than T-bills. But because almost every diversified equity portfolio has b near 1, it says little about how to choose among equity portfolios. That choice is made through s and h.

As a DFA representative, I would argue that macroeconomic variables are the wrong tool for several reasons. First, they are measured poorly. GDP, inflation and industrial production are released infrequently, revised and noisy, while SMB and HML are tradable returns observed every month, and the model explains over 90% of return variation. Second, theory does not tell us which macro variables are priced, with what sign, or with what stability, so choosing among them invites exactly the data mining we are trying to avoid. Third, we can build portfolios that target size and BE/ME exposure directly and measure it precisely, but we cannot hold a portfolio "long GDP growth." The factor regressions also let clients evaluate any manager through the intercept and its t-statistic (Exhs. 14–16). Fourth, macro-based models need economic forecasts, which is active management, and DFA believes prices already reflect public information. Finally, we accept that the economic source of the SMB and HML risks is not fully identified (the Addendum calls this "the challenge in the future"), but factor portfolios capture whatever the true underlying state variables are, so we can earn the premium without naming them.

## 10. If size and value are tied to risk, why pay DFA?

Efficient markets does not mean every stock has the same expected return. It means there are no *risk-adjusted* excess returns from stock picking or timing, and that expected return follows risk. DFA's philosophy is exactly that investors are rewarded for the risks they bear and that no one can consistently beat the market on a risk-adjusted basis. So DFA does not claim that its portfolios beat the market for free. It claims that they offer different risk exposures, and that many investors should want more of them than the market portfolio gives.

The Addendum says that in the three-factor world "a market portfolio such as an S&P 500 index fund no longer plays any special role," because it is weighted toward large, low-BE/ME stocks. Investors differ in how much distress risk they can bear, so long-horizon, tax-exempt investors such as pensions and endowments, who are DFA's main clients, can reasonably tilt toward small and value stocks for higher expected returns. This is a risk choice and not a bet against efficiency. The Addendum's blended portfolios illustrate it: moving from the S&P 500 to 40% S&P, 40% big high-BE/ME, 10% small and 10% small high-BE/ME raises the annual return from 9.9% to 13.0% while the standard deviation rises only from 16.5% to 17.5% (Addendum Exh. 6).

What DFA sells beyond the tilt is implementation. It holds broad, diversified portfolios with pure exposure, avoids paying for fundamental research, and keeps turnover low. Exhibits 14–16 show that most active managers drift across size and value, while DFA's portfolios sit where intended. Its trading earns a return for providing liquidity to sellers, which is compensation for a service and consistent with market efficiency, and DFA reports about 200 basis points a year of outperformance against standard small-stock indexes from this alone. Its fees of 35–50 basis points are well below the 69–85 basis points of the median active small-cap fund (2002 Ex. 5), and DFA avoids index-style products where it cannot add value. The same logic carries over to its tax-managed funds, international value funds and advisor distribution.

There are limits. Small and value stocks lagged for years in 1982–93 and in the late 1990s, and HML's return of over 80% in 2000–01 is what kept clients loyal. If the premia are behavioral rather than risk-based, they could also be competed away as more money pursues them. DFA's answer is that its clients understand the risk they hold, and that its cost and discipline advantages remain in either case.
