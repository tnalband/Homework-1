## 7. Size and value strategies: rational vs. behavioral views

**The evidence.** Small stocks have beaten large stocks over long periods: 1942–91 small (deciles 9–10) returned 16.2% a year versus 12.9% for the S&P 500 (Ex. 3). Banz documented this for 1926–late 1970s (2002 case). Fama–French found that high BE/ME stocks beat low BE/ME stocks in 1963–90 and that beta has no explanatory power for average returns. In 1964–91, small high-BE/ME stocks returned 18.0% a year versus 10.2% for small low-BE/ME stocks, and large high-BE/ME stocks returned 14.3% versus 9.8% for large low-BE/ME stocks (Ex. 11). The effect also held in 1926–62 data hand-collected by Fama, French and Davis, and in nearly every country in the MSCI data (2002 case).

**What efficient-market theorists say.** In an efficient market, a persistent excess return is compensation for risk. Beta does not explain these premia, so size and book-to-market must proxy for other priced risks.

- *Value.* High BE/ME firms are relatively distressed. Their earnings on book equity are low and fall until the ranking year, while low BE/ME firms are consistently profitable (Ex. 10, Addendum Exhs. 2–3). Small, high-BE/ME firms actually lose money on average in some years. Investors must therefore bear the risk of a distress state in which these firms fail together. Fama and French found that value–growth returns are correlated across countries. Value therefore cannot be diversified away internationally, and there were years when growth beat value in many countries (2002 case).
- *Size.* Small firms have more volatile earnings and weaker profitability (Addendum Exh. 3). Small stocks also carry higher standard deviations (27.3% for CRSP 6–10 versus 16.1% for the S&P 500, Ex. 11).
- *The premia are not constant.* Small stocks lagged in 1982–91 (SMB averaged –0.9% in 1982–93, 2002 Ex. 6). Value lagged in the late 1990s and then returned about 80% on HML in 2000–01. A premium that sometimes disappears for years is what a risk premium looks like.

**What behavioralists say.** Prices deviate from fundamentals because investors have common biases, and the premia reward the other side of those biases.

- *Value.* Investors extrapolate past growth and overpay for "glamour" stocks, then are disappointed. This is why low BE/ME stocks are called "growth" or "glamour" stocks and high BE/ME stocks are called "value" stocks. The 2002 case notes that "new theories… used evidence from psychology."
- *Size.* Small firms are neglected, under-researched and illiquid. Some argue the effect is data mining, or that it disappeared once it became known. The 1982–93 drought is cited as evidence.
- Behavioralists point out that value stocks were not riskier by volatility: small high-BE/ME stocks had a 26.0% standard deviation versus 30.0% for low BE/ME, and large high BE/ME 17.3% versus 17.9% (Ex. 11). Higher returns with no higher volatility look like a mispricing.

**What DFA believes.** DFA takes the risk view. The 1993 Addendum says high-BE/ME firms are better described as "stressed" or "stretched" than as "value," and low-BE/ME firms as "healthy," because the premium is a reward for distress risk. The 2002 case says DFA disliked the "glamour" label because "the idea that low BE/ME stocks performed poorly because they were overhyped… did not fit its efficient markets views." DFA concedes that the underlying risk factor "has yet to be identified" (Addendum). DFA does not need the behavioral story to be wrong in order to sell the strategy. It only needs the premia to persist and the risk to be acceptable to its clients.

## 8. Trading costs of small, value stocks and how DFA manages them

**The frictions.**
- *Bid–ask spreads and market impact.* The spread is 0.48% for decile 1 and 11.32% for decile 10 (Ex. 4), over 23 times larger. Average daily dollar volume per issue is $41k in decile 10 versus $25.8m in decile 1.
- *Price pressure.* A buyer moves the price. For block trades, the average seller's cost is 2.94% (Ex. 5).
- *Adverse selection.* The seller may know something the market does not (the "lemons problem").
- *Information leakage.* If the seller dumps the rest of the position after DFA buys, the price falls.
- *Capacity and turnover.* DFA owned over 3% of the smallest two deciles and 6–9% of many individual stocks, and over 5% of many small firms in 2002. Stocks migrate across size and BE/ME boundaries, which forces trades.
- *Tax-managed funds.* Value stocks pay high dividends (2002 case).

**How DFA manages them.**
1. *Be a liquidity provider, not a liquidity taker.* DFA absorbs large blocks from impatient sellers (it screened about 600 inquiries a day in 1993) and demands a discount instead of paying a spread. In 2001, 36% of small-cap purchases were block trades at a –3.33% cost (a 3.33% discount), versus +0.58% for orders worked in the market. The overall cost was –0.83%, a net gain (2002 Ex. 10). DFA reports that this adds about 200 bp a year over small-stock indexes.
2. *Control adverse selection without fundamental research.* DFA avoids stocks with imminent earnings announcements, recent insider sales or pending news. It trades with reputable brokers and uses a "penalty box" for brokers who withhold information. It requires the seller's entire position to be offered, so no further sale follows. Its reputation for secrecy gives sellers confidence.
3. *Hold ranges.* Exhibit 7 shows buy, hold and sell bands. DFA sells a stock only after it has strayed well out of its target range, which lowers turnover and costs.
4. *Patience and balance.* DFA does not hold exact index weights. It will buy more of an already overweight stock only for a bigger discount, which keeps tracking error low. When selling it offers under 25% of daily volume. A stock that outgrows the Micro Cap Portfolio can be sold to the Small XM Portfolio. Large redemptions are paid in kind.
5. *Fees.* DFA charges 35–50 bp (Ex. 5, 2002), against 69–85 bp for the median small-cap fund. It argues the trading discount more than pays for the fee.

**Caveat.** The discount is shrinking as DFA grows. The share of purchases made at a discount fell from 80% in 1992 to 36% in 2001. Capacity is limited, which is why DFA pushed larger clients toward the 6-7-8 strategy.

## 9. The Fama–French three-factor model

**The model.**
R<sub>i</sub> – R<sub>f</sub> = a + b·(R<sub>M</sub> – R<sub>f</sub>) + s·SMB + h·HML + e

SMB is small minus big and HML is high minus low BE/ME. The slopes b, s and h are the portfolio's exposures to market, size and value risk. The market factor drives the overall equity decision. In Addendum Exhibit 4, 25 size/BE/ME portfolios all have b near 1.0, so market beta barely separates them. The size slope s falls from about +1.2 for the smallest stocks to about –0.15 for the largest. The h slope runs from negative for low BE/ME to +0.6 to +0.7 for high BE/ME. R² is 0.84–0.96, and 23 of the 25 intercepts are statistically zero. The three factors therefore explain most of both the variation and the average level of returns.

**The rational argument.** If factor exposures explain average returns and are tied to real economic risk, the premia are fair compensation. SMB and HML are risk factors because they explain common variation in returns that diversification cannot remove. Small and high-BE/ME firms share earnings distress (Ex. 10, Addendum Exh. 2). The pattern also appears in 1926–62 data and across countries, which makes data mining unlikely.

**Is beta useful?** Not for the cross-section. Beta alone has no relation to average returns, and after adjusting for size, low-beta stocks earn more than high-beta stocks (Appendix A1). Beta still matters within the model, as the market exposure. It governs how much to hold in equities versus T-bills, and it is the market component of each portfolio. Because most diversified portfolios have b ≈ 1, it tells an investor little about how to *choose among* equity portfolios. The investor chooses s and h.

**Why DFA does not use macroeconomic variables.** As a DFA representative, I would make five points.
1. *Measurement.* Macro variables such as GDP, inflation and industrial production are released infrequently and are revised or noisy. SMB and HML are tradable portfolio returns observed monthly. The three-factor model's R² of 0.85–0.96 is better than we can get from macro variables.
2. *Which variable?* Theory does not tell us which macro variables are priced or with what sign, and the effect of macro news on stock prices changes over time. That would invite the very data mining we are trying to avoid.
3. *Implementation.* We can build portfolios that target size and BE/ME exposure directly and measure exposure precisely. We cannot hold a portfolio "long GDP growth." Style analysis (Exhs. 14–16) lets us evaluate any manager by their intercept *a* and t-statistic.
4. *Philosophy.* Macro-based models need forecasts of the economy, which is active management. DFA believes prices already reflect public information.
5. *Honesty.* We accept that the underlying economic source of the SMB/HML risk is not fully identified (Addendum: "the challenge in the future will be to identify these underlying risk factors"). Factor-mimicking portfolios capture whatever the true state variables are, so it is not essential to name them to earn the premium.

## 10. If size and value are tied to risk, why pay DFA?

Efficient markets does not mean that all stocks have the same expected return. It means that there are no *risk-adjusted* excess returns available from stock picking or timing, and that expected return follows risk. DFA's own investment philosophy is that investors are rewarded for risks they bear and that nobody can consistently beat the market on a risk-adjusted basis. DFA's justification has four parts.

1. **The market portfolio is not the only sensible portfolio.** The Addendum's position is that "a market portfolio such as an S&P 500 index fund no longer plays any special role." The S&P 500 is concentrated in big, low-BE/ME stocks. Investors differ in their willingness to bear distress risk, so those with a long horizon and no taxes, such as pensions and endowments (DFA's main clients), can sensibly tilt to small and value for higher expected returns. Tilting is a risk decision, not a bet against efficiency. The Addendum's blended-portfolio table shows how an investor can increase small and high-BE/ME exposure: moving from the S&P 500 to 40% S&P / 40% BH / 10% D6-10 / 10% SH raises returns from 9.9% to 13.0% for a standard deviation of 16.5% to 17.5% (Addendum Exh. 6).
2. **Implementation is where DFA earns its fee.** DFA is not a stock picker. It adds value by capturing the factor premia cheaply and consistently. Its lack of fundamental research keeps costs and turnover low, and it earns a *service* return for supplying liquidity (discounts on block trades). That is compensation for a service and is consistent with efficiency. DFA reports about 200 bp a year of outperformance against standard small-stock indexes, and its fees (35–50 bp) are below those of the median active small-cap fund (about 70–85 bp). DFA avoids cheap index products where it cannot add value.
3. **Diversification and discipline.** DFA holds broad sector portfolios rather than concentrated bets, and it keeps style pure. Exhibits 14–16 show most active managers drift widely across size and value, while DFA's portfolios sit at the desired exposure.
4. **Extensions that fit the same logic.** Tax-managed funds, international value funds and advisor channels (RIAs) apply the same ideas: low cost, diversification, academic research.

**Limits.** The strategy carries real risk: small and value lagged for much of 1982–93 and in the 1990s, and DFA's index-like products compete against cheaper index funds and ETFs (2012 case). If the premia are behavioral rather than risk-based, they are also exposed to being competed away as more money chases them. DFA's answer is that clients who understand the risk will stay, and 2000–01 supported that view when HML returned over 80%.
